I want to describe the world I actually believe we are heading toward,
because every technical argument in this essay exists in service of it.

Start with the gramophone. In 2005, a gramophone was not a broken object. It
worked. It played music, faithfully, to anyone who wanted to hear music. What
killed it was not a better gramophone. It was a format change that made its
entire physical form pointless — the needle, the groove, the horn, the
furniture-sized cabinet, all of it existed to solve a problem that had
stopped existing.

Screens are gramophones.

A screen is a window you look into. Every screen ever manufactured is the
same compromise: take a three-dimensional world, flatten it onto a plane, and
ask a brain evolved for depth, parallax, and presence to accept the
flattening. We have become extraordinarily good at that compromise — 4K, HDR,
120 hertz, quantum dot, OLED — decades of brilliant engineering spent making
a lossy translation less noticeable. The translation is about to stop being
necessary.

Before describing what replaces it, here is the single thing this essay
optimizes for, because every architectural decision in it follows from one
objective:

<Axiom n="1">
  Drive the power requirement of every device down far enough that it can be
  powered wirelessly. Everything else — computation moving off the body,
  inference nodes in buildings, deciding what to transmit versus what to
  reconstruct, robots buffering their own power spikes — is a means to that
  one end. Each is a way of removing work from the device, and every unit of
  work removed is a unit of power the device no longer has to carry.
</Axiom>

Get the demand low enough and something changes in kind rather than in
degree: the device stops needing stored energy at all. It draws what it needs
from the space around it, continuously, the way it already draws Wi-Fi. No
plugging in. No docking. No charging overnight.

That is the end state — a wirelessly powered world — and the rest of this
essay is the architecture that gets there.

One clarification on scope. I started by asking how to power better phones
and laptops, and it was the wrong question. Those devices are the current
form of a compromise that is ending; designing a power architecture for them
is designing for a category on its way out. The devices worth designing for
are the ones that come next: no screens, no batteries, sensing on the body,
intelligence in the environment, worlds rendered around you rather than
displayed in front of you.

So, what replaces the screen. A recorded moment today becomes footage: a
rectangle you watch, from the position the camera happened to occupy. In the
world this essay describes, you do not watch it. You stand inside it. Video
calls stop being a grid of rectangles containing faces — you meet somewhere,
a landscape or a workshop generated on demand, specific to that meeting. A
colleague on the other side of the planet stands beside you at the machine
you are both trying to fix, casting light-consistent shadows, pointing at the
part they mean.

Presence is not a resolution problem. What convinces the brain is not pixel
density — it is latency short enough to be imperceptible, parallax that
responds correctly to your head, occlusion that behaves, sound arriving from
the right direction, lighting consistent with the geometry you are standing
in. Get those aligned and the brain stops auditing the experience and simply
accepts it. It does not require perfect fidelity. It requires *coherent*
fidelity.

And none of it plugs in. The devices that deliver this draw milliwatts to a
few watts, harvested continuously from the field around them, supplied by
localized emitters in buildings and, eventually, by solar collectors in orbit
beaming power to the ground.

## 1. The last generation to look at screens

Here is the consequence I think is most underrated. **The battery is the most
tyrannical constraint in modern industrial design.** Almost every consumer
device of the last thirty years is, structurally, a box built around a cell.
Thickness, weight, weight distribution, thermal layout, silhouette, materials
— the entire visual language of consumer electronics is downstream of a
rectangular lithium brick and the heat it produces.

Remove it and industrial design gets its freedom back for the first time
since the transistor. Glasses that look like glasses, because they weigh what
glasses weigh. Objects that compute and look like jewelry, like fabric, like
nothing in particular. Forms determined by who the object is for and what it
does, rather than by what has to fit inside it.

That is a creative explosion, and it follows a pattern we have seen before.
When a constraint that shaped an entire category lifts, the first decade is
chaos and delight — it happened when synthesizers freed music from what a
body could physically play, when digital cameras freed photography from the
cost per frame.

<MarginNote label="scale">
  This is what abundance looks like at the scale of a Kardashev Type I
  civilization, and the threshold is less exotic than it sounds. It does not
  mean starships. It means energy so available that it stops being a design
  consideration at all — the way clean water stopped being one in a modern
  city, the way bandwidth stopped being one for most of what we do online.
  Ambient energy across a room and orbital energy across a continent are the
  same idea, and both are steps along the same path.
</MarginNote>

I find this future overwhelmingly optimistic, and closer than it looks, for a
specific reason: not one piece of it requires a scientific miracle. Every
component is an engineering problem, most of them already have people working
on them for entirely unrelated reasons, and the funding is already flowing.
None of it exists yet. The reason is narrower and more specific than almost
anyone assumes.

## 2. The battery is the last unsolved systems problem

Every other component of modern computing has improved by orders of
magnitude. One has not.

That is an improvement of a few percent a year, compounding slowly, bounded
by electrochemistry rather than lithography. There is no Moore's Law for
electrons in a cell, because the constraint is not pattern resolution. It is
the periodic table.

The consequence is that the battery has quietly become the organizing
principle of hardware design. Phones ship with thermal throttling because
sustained compute drains the cell faster than it can be replenished.
Wearables run stripped-down processors not because engineers lack better
silicon, but because better silicon would empty the battery before lunch.
Laptops perform differently on battery than on mains power, which means the
same machine is two different computers depending on where its electrons come
from.

We have normalized this. We treat "battery life" as a product specification
rather than as evidence of an unsolved engineering problem. The interesting
question is not how to make batteries better. The interesting question is:

<Axiom n="2">
  What would computing look like if we stopped designing around the battery
  entirely? That question turns out to reorganize almost everything
  downstream of it.
</Axiom>

## 3. Computing evolves by moving bottlenecks

The history of computing is not a history of solved problems. It is a
history of relocated ones.

Mainframes were bottlenecked by physical access. Solution: put the computer
on the desk. Personal computers were bottlenecked by isolation. Solution:
network them. Networked PCs were bottlenecked by local storage and
maintenance. Solution: move both to the cloud. Cloud-dependent devices were
bottlenecked by connectivity. Solution: mobile broadband. Mobile devices were
bottlenecked by their own processing limits. Solution: server-side inference.

At no point did anyone eliminate a constraint. They moved computation to
wherever the constraint was cheapest to satisfy. Streaming is the cleanest
example: music did not solve local storage, it relocated storage to a data
center and paid for that relocation in bandwidth, which was falling in cost
faster than storage was growing in capacity. The iPod's entire value
proposition — carry your library with you — was dissolved not by a better
iPod but by an architectural decision that made the question irrelevant.

This is the pattern worth internalizing: each generation asks *which resource
is getting cheaper fastest, and what can I move onto it?* Right now, two
resources are getting dramatically cheaper: distributed computation and
wireless bandwidth. One resource is nearly static: portable stored energy.

**If energy storage is the slow curve, move work off the things that carry
stored energy.** Not by making devices more efficient at the margin, but by
relocating the energy-hungry functions to places where energy is effectively
unlimited. The next migration in computing is not a faster processor. It is
the migration of computation and energy away from the body.

## 4. Compute does not belong on the human body

Wearables inherited their architecture by accident. When smartwatches and
headsets were designed, the only reference architecture available was the
smartphone: a self-contained unit that senses, computes, renders,
communicates, and stores its own power. That model was correct for phones. It
was copied onto the body without anyone asking whether it should be.

The result is a category of devices asked to do five jobs simultaneously, in
a thermal envelope constrained by human skin, powered by a cell constrained
by human tolerance for weight. A modern mixed-reality headset performs
simultaneous localization and mapping, hand and eye tracking, scene
understanding, neural rendering, and high-bandwidth radio, all while
remaining light enough to wear and cool enough to sit against a face. Every
one of those requirements fights the others, and as models grow larger and
more capable, the architecture becomes untenable — the compute demanded by
useful spatial intelligence is rising faster than the energy budget available
on a human head, and there is no version of that curve where the head wins.

<Axiom n="3">
  The device on your body should be a sensor and a display. Nothing else. Its
  job is to capture what the world looks like from your position, transmit
  that efficiently, receive a rendered or partially rendered result, and put
  it in front of your eyes.
</Axiom>

Cameras, IMUs, microphones, eye trackers, and a radio can all run in the
milliwatt range with existing silicon. What cannot run in the milliwatt range
is inference and rendering — so those should not be there. Glasses that weigh
thirty grams and last all day are not a distant materials-science
achievement. They are an architectural decision that has not yet been made at
scale.

## 5. The shift from geometry to intent

If rendering moves off the device, something has to travel over the air. The
question of what travels is the central technical question of the spatial
internet, and both obvious answers are wrong.

**Send the geometry.** Capture the world volumetrically, compress it, stream
it. Conceptually simple, and enormously expensive: raw volumetric and
light-field capture runs into gigabits per second before compression, and
fidelity scales with data, and human vision is demanding.

**Send the meaning.** Transmit a compact semantic description — this is a
room, there is a person, they are moving this way — and let a generative
model reconstruct the scene at the receiving end. Bandwidth collapses to
almost nothing, but the entire cost transfers to inference: power, heat,
latency, and a reconstruction that is plausible rather than accurate. Fine
for entertainment. Not fine for a surgeon, an engineer, or anyone whose
decisions depend on the world being exactly as it is.

Both answers optimize the wrong variable. One minimizes computation. The
other minimizes transmission. Neither minimizes what actually constrains the
system.

> Transmit only the information whose transmission costs less energy than
> reconstructing it locally.

That reframing turns a format debate into an optimization problem. Geometry,
semantic descriptors, latent features, raw sensor measurements, and cached
priors are not competing standards. They are interchangeable tools inside one
energy budget. A static wall that has not changed in an hour should not be
transmitted — it should be recalled. A face mid-expression should be
transmitted at high fidelity, because generating it is both more expensive
and less trustworthy. A texture the receiver has seen a thousand times should
be a pointer, not a payload.

The objective function is not bits. It is total energy consumed across
communication, computation, memory, and rendering, subject to perceptual
fidelity and real-time responsiveness. This matters because the trade-off
does not stabilize: 6G will relax bandwidth constraints without eliminating
them, better inference silicon will lower computation costs without zeroing
them, and the ratio between "cheaper to send" and "cheaper to generate" keeps
shifting by generation, location, time of day, and whether you are in a
stadium or a forest. **The architecture cannot pick a side. It has to
negotiate the split dynamically, per scene, per frame, per object.** That
negotiation layer is the piece nobody has built yet, and it matters more than
any individual codec or model.

## 6. The rise of distributed intelligence

If the device stops generating worlds, the generation has to happen
somewhere. There are two default answers, and both fail.

**The cloud fails on physics.** Comfortable spatial computing requires
motion-to-photon latency under roughly twenty milliseconds. Light in fibre
travels around 200,000 kilometres per second, so a round trip of a thousand
kilometres costs about ten milliseconds before a single packet is queued,
processed, or rendered. Hyperscale data centers are built where land and
power are cheap, which is almost by definition far from where people are. The
speed of light is not an engineering problem to be optimized. It is a
boundary condition.

**The local device fails on energy.** That was Section 4.

What sits between them is not a compromise. It is a new layer: **localized
micro data centers positioned within a few kilometres of users.** Cabinets,
not campuses. Enough compute to render for a neighbourhood, close enough that
the round trip is a millisecond or two, connected to mains power and to real
cooling, and — this is the part that matters — shared across everyone in
range.

Sharing is what makes the economics work. A headset's GPU is idle most of the
time and sized for peak load. A neighbourhood inference node runs at high
utilization because human activity is statistically smooth in aggregate. The
same silicon serves a hundred users at a fraction of the per-user cost, in a
location where cooling is a fan rather than a thermal-comfort constraint
against someone's forehead.

Once that layer exists, the hierarchy inverts. The node becomes the
computer. The glasses, the watch, the phone, the car, the robot — all become
interfaces to it.

## 7. Spatial computing becomes infrastructure

The moment computation becomes room-scale rather than body-scale, it stops
being a consumer electronics problem and becomes a **building** problem.

This transition has happened before, repeatedly, and it always looks the
same. Electricity began as a per-device consideration and became a wall
socket. Networking began as a cable you ran yourself and became a property
feature. Air conditioning began as a machine you bought and became a system
you inhabit. In each case, the technology stopped being a product you carried
and became a service the space provided.

Compute is next. An office already has an electrical plan, a network plan,
and an HVAC plan. It will acquire a compute plan: where the inference nodes
live, how they are cooled, how much power they draw, how coverage is
distributed so that no room is a dead zone. A hospital will specify
computational coverage in an operating theatre the way it currently specifies
gas lines and sterile airflow. Real estate developers will advertise it.
Building codes will eventually address it. Landlords will meter it.

And the room, having acquired sensors and inference and memory of its own
state, becomes computationally intelligent — not in a mystical sense, but in
the ordinary sense that it can perceive its own geometry and occupancy and
serve that understanding to whatever devices are present. The wearable stops
being a computer you wear and becomes a key you carry.

Now the argument closes on itself, because relocating computation relocates
energy demand. The principle is simple:

Read that hierarchy against the history of wearable design and the mistake
becomes obvious: we have spent a decade trying to fit watt-scale computation
into a milliwatt-scale power envelope, and calling the resulting compromise a
product. The alternative is not a better battery. It is a smaller problem.

**Instead of making batteries larger, minimize what requires a battery at
all.** If the body-worn device draws single-digit milliwatts averaged over a
day, the entire energy conversation changes character. You are no longer
asking how to store enough energy for eight hours of GPU work. You are asking
how to supply a few milliwatts continuously to something that never has to be
plugged in. Those are not the same question, and the second one has answers
the first one does not. Energy follows computation. Move the computation, and
the energy problem moves with it — into buildings, where it was already
solved.

## 9. Ambient power instead of managed power

Here is where wireless power stops being a gimmick.

Wireless power today is mostly point-to-point: a charging pad with a coil, or
a beam-steering system that tracks a device and delivers watts to it
directly. Both are attempts to replace the cord. Both inherit the cord's
mental model — a dedicated link between a source and a sink, negotiated,
exclusive, and efficiency-obsessed, because when you are moving watts, every
percentage point of loss is heat and cost.

That entire framing dissolves once device demand collapses into the
milliwatt range. At milliwatt scale, the objective is no longer efficient
beam delivery. It is **coverage**. Energy stops being a link and becomes a
field: continuously present, broadly distributed, harvested opportunistically
by any device inside it, shared simultaneously by every device in range
without negotiation or handshake.

The right analogy is not the power cord. It is radio. Nobody asks whether an
FM signal is efficiently delivered to their car. The transmitter radiates,
and anyone within coverage receives. The system is enormously wasteful by
point-to-point standards and completely successful by its own, because the
value is in ubiquity rather than in efficiency. Ambient power works the same
way — a transmitter saturating a volume with RF at safe exposure limits
delivers a low but reliable power density everywhere inside it. Ten devices
in the room do not divide a budget; they each take what they can capture.
Adding a device costs the infrastructure nothing.

Honest accounting matters here. Ambient RF harvesting from existing broadcast
and cellular signals yields microwatts at best. Purpose-built ambient power
systems can do considerably better within safety limits, but the delivered
power density still falls off with distance, and the end-to-end efficiency of
such a system, measured conventionally, is poor. The response to that is not
to dispute the numbers. It is to reject the metric.

<Axiom n="4">
  Efficiency is the wrong measure for infrastructure whose value is coverage.
  The grid loses a meaningful fraction of generated power in transmission and
  distribution, and no one considers this an argument against
  electrification. A city-scale ambient power layer supplying billions of
  milliwatt devices consumes a rounding error against the same city's air
  conditioning load. That is the correct comparison, and it favours ambient
  power decisively.
</Axiom>

## 10. The four converging curves

No single breakthrough produces this architecture. It emerges from four
independent trends, none of which were created for this purpose.

**Curve one — falling device power.** Sensor front ends, low-power radios,
always-on wake circuits, and near-threshold silicon have driven the standby
and duty-cycled power of a capable sensing device down by orders of magnitude
over two decades, driven by the IoT and mobile industries for reasons
unrelated to this argument.

**Curve two — improving harvesting efficiency.** RF-to-DC rectifier design,
low-voltage cold-start circuits, and multi-band antenna design have steadily
improved the fraction of a weak field convertible into usable power, driven
largely by RFID, medical implants, and industrial sensing.

**Curve three — compute decentralization.** Edge inference silicon, telecom
edge deployments, and the economics of latency-sensitive services are pushing
computation outward from hyperscale centers toward users, funded by the
entire telecommunications and cloud industry for reasons entirely unrelated
to wearables.

**Curve four — falling launch costs.** Reusable launch has cut the cost of
putting mass into orbit by roughly an order of magnitude within a generation,
with further reductions plausible, driven by satellite constellations and
national space programs.

Each curve is independently financed, independently motivated, and
independently on track. The systems argument is that they multiply rather
than add: device power falling by three orders of magnitude and harvesting
efficiency improving by one means the gap between what a device needs and
what a field can supply closes by four orders — and that gap closing is the
entire precondition for battery-free operation. Nobody is building toward
this convergence. It is happening as a byproduct, which is the most reliable
kind of technological progress there is.

## 11. Why AI accidentally solves wireless power

The AI infrastructure buildout looks like the opposite of a low-power future.
Gigawatt data centers, dedicated power plants, a capital expenditure race
measured in hundreds of billions of dollars — it reads as a story about
energy consumption, not energy liberation.

It is, in fact, the single strongest enabling force for battery-free devices,
and the mechanism is almost too simple to notice: **every dollar spent making
remote inference cheap and fast reduces the amount of computation that must
happen locally.** The industry is building, at enormous scale and for
entirely commercial reasons, the exact infrastructure this architecture
requires — dense inference capacity, low-latency distribution, model serving
at scale. Nobody in that industry is trying to eliminate batteries. They are
trying to serve models profitably. But the second-order effect of succeeding
is that the computational floor for a client device keeps dropping — and the
lower that floor falls, the closer it gets to what an ambient field can
supply without any battery at all.

This is how infrastructure transitions usually work. The enabling investment
is made for an unrelated reason, by people with no interest in the eventual
application, and the application arrives later to find its foundation already
poured.

## 12. The spatial internet

Put the pieces together and the result is not a better headset. It is a
different internet.

The internet we have is visual and symbolic — information lives inside
rectangles, navigated through metaphors invented to make an abstract system
legible on a two-dimensional screen. The spatial internet inverts that:
persistent three-dimensional environments, continuously generated around
you, anchored to real locations, shared with other people who are physically
or virtually present. Information does not live in a rectangle. It lives
where it is relevant.

Three shifts follow. The internet becomes spatial rather than visual —
content has position, persistence, and physical relationship rather than URL
and page rank. Interaction becomes physical rather than symbolic — you reach,
move, look, and speak, and the translation layer between intention and
action thins. Interfaces disappear into environments — the most successful
interface is the one nobody notices, because the room simply behaves
correctly.

This is only possible under the architecture described above. A spatial
internet running on self-contained battery-powered devices is a demo. A
spatial internet running on ambient power and distributed inference is
infrastructure.

## 13. Robotics changes the equation again

Robots are currently designed exactly the way wearables are: as complete
self-contained computers that carry their own perception, planning,
inference, and power. That design faces the same constraint, amplified. A
humanoid robot spends a substantial fraction of its energy budget carrying
the mass of its own battery and computer, and a further fraction cooling
that computer. Every watt of onboard inference is a watt not available for
actuation, and every kilogram of battery is a kilogram of payload lost.

Once ambient sensing, distributed inference, and spatial intelligence
mature, robots inherit the architecture for the same reason wearables do. A
robot operating inside a computationally instrumented building does not need
to build a map — the building has one. It becomes an **embodied endpoint**:
actuators, sensors, safety-critical local reflexes, and a fast link to the
infrastructure that handles everything above the reflex layer. Local autonomy
does not disappear — anything safety-critical stays on board, because a
robot that stops thinking when the network hiccups is not a product — but the
split between what must be local and what should be infrastructural is an
engineering decision, and current designs make it in the wrong place by
default.

*The battery backpack is the wrong answer.* The industry's response to
robot endurance has been to pursue better chemistry — the goal is a better
battery backpack. This treats the symptom. A physical agent that requires
tens of kilograms of stored energy to operate for a few hours has already
lost the architecture argument twice: once because it spends a meaningful
share of its energy carrying and cooling its own power supply, and again
because that mass degrades every mechanical parameter that matters.

It also forecloses the endgame. A robot with a high continuous power draw
cannot be powered wirelessly, and the reason is not engineering difficulty.
It is safety. Delivering kilowatts continuously to a moving target in a
public space requires field intensities far above human exposure limits — at
those densities you have not built power infrastructure, you have built a
directed-energy weapon and pointed it down a sidewalk. Exposure limits are
not a regulatory inconvenience to be lobbied away. They are the hard ceiling
the entire architecture has to fit under.

*Trickle-spike.* Once you accept the ceiling as fixed, the design problem
inverts. Stop asking how much power can be transmitted. Start with what can
safely be harvested, and build the machine that fits inside it. The baseline
available to a wirelessly powered robot is governed by a first-principles
relationship:

$$
P_{\text{harvested}} = S \times A \times \eta
$$

— incident power density, effective receiving area, and conversion
efficiency. Three terms, and only one of them is locked. Power density is
capped by safety and sits wherever exposure standards put it; no amount of
transmitter engineering moves it. Effective area is a design variable, and a
larger one than it appears: conformal metamaterial rectennas remove the
assumption that a robot needs to carry a flat panel — the receiving surface
becomes the skin of the machine, curved, distributed across torso and limbs.
Conversion efficiency is an engineering variable, improving for reasons
unrelated to robotics.

Set against that harvestable baseline is the demand side, and two levers
matter there far more than any specific technology: **compute that costs
less** — neuromorphic silicon, event-driven perception, aggressive offload of
everything above the reflex layer — and **actuation that fights physics
less** — passive dynamics, elastic energy storage, compliant mechanisms,
bio-inspired designs that exploit gravity and momentum instead of
brute-forcing every joint with an oversized motor. Biological locomotion is
efficient because it spends most of its cycle falling and catching.

Even with demand driven down, robots have a load profile problem: locomotion
is not a flat draw. Standing, sensing, and slow movement sit near the
baseline. Jumping, lifting, catching, and sprinting spike an order of
magnitude above it, for fractions of a second. Sizing a power system for
those spikes is what produces the battery backpack. But the spikes do not
need to be *supplied* by the harvest. They need to be *buffered*.

> Harvest the baseline continuously and wirelessly. Buffer the peaks locally
> in onboard storage.

Solid-state supercapacitors are well suited to exactly this role — high
power density, fast discharge, high cycle life, and indifference to the
deep-discharge behavior that degrades chemical cells. Paired with a small
battery for reserve, they absorb the spike and recharge from the trickle
during the far longer intervals between spikes. The energy balance is
straightforward: over any duty cycle, harvested energy must meet or exceed
consumed energy. Storage decouples instantaneous supply from instantaneous
demand.

**Onboard storage stops being sized for endurance and becomes sized for the
largest single burst.** Endurance comes from the field. Storage handles
transients. Those are different components, orders of magnitude apart in
capacity, and conflating them is what puts a battery backpack on every
humanoid in development. That distinction is the entire argument. The
physical world becomes the operating system, and robots become one of
several classes of process running on it.

## 14. Beyond Earth: orbital energy infrastructure

Space-based solar power has been proposed since the 1960s and has failed on
economics every time. The physics was always sound: a collector in orbit
receives sunlight continuously, without atmosphere, weather, or night, at
roughly a third more intensity than the surface. The problem was that
lifting a power station cost more than the power was worth, by a margin no
efficiency improvement could close.

Launch cost is the only variable that ever mattered, and it is the one that
moved. Reusable launch has already cut cost-per-kilogram to orbit by an
order of magnitude, and further reductions are credible if not yet
demonstrated. Meanwhile, the transmission side has moved from theory to
hardware: orbital demonstrations have beamed measurable power to receivers on
the ground. The quantities are small — this is early-stage work — but the
loop has been closed, and closing a loop is the hard part.

The framing that matters: **orbital power is not a separate technology. It
is the same ambient architecture at a different altitude.** A terrestrial
ambient power layer and an orbital one solve the same problem — delivering
energy to distributed receivers without physical connection — with the same
fundamental method. The receiver technology overlaps. The regulatory
questions rhyme. The system design principles are identical. One serves a
room; one serves a region. This is why orbital power belongs in this
argument rather than in a separate speculative appendix: it is the outermost
layer of the same stack, and it becomes plausible for a reason unrelated to
itself — launch economics improved because of satellite internet, and
orbital power gets the benefit.

```
   Spatial Intelligence
       ↓
   Distributed Edge Compute
       ↓
   Ambient Energy Infrastructure
```
</Schematic>

The distinction is not cosmetic. In the first stack, the unit of computing is
a device, bounded by what it can carry. In the second, the unit of computing
is an environment, bounded by what the grid can deliver — which is to say,
not meaningfully bounded at all. Note what happened to the bottom layer. It
did not get better. It got *replaced by infrastructure*. That is the same
move electrification made when it replaced on-site generators with a grid,
and the same move cloud computing made when it replaced server closets with
regions. In both cases the winning architecture was not a superior version of
the constrained component. It was the removal of the component from the
critical path.

## 16. Capital always builds the future before it understands it

Infrastructure consistently arrives before the application that justifies
it, built by people who are wrong about what they are building. Railroads
were financed for freight and reorganized where people lived. The electrical
grid was built to sell lighting and enabled the assembly line, refrigeration,
and everything downstream of the electric motor. Fiber laid during the
telecom bubble was justified by demand forecasts that were badly wrong, went
dark, sold for cents on the dollar, and then carried streaming video and the
cloud. Data centers built for enterprise virtualization became the substrate
for everything that followed. The AI buildout underway now is justified by
revenue projections that will look either naive or timid in a decade, and
either way the concrete and copper will remain.

The pattern: capital builds capacity for a stated reason, the stated reason
turns out to be incomplete, and the capacity gets used for something its
financiers did not anticipate. Every component of the architecture in this
essay is already being financed by someone with an unrelated motive. Edge
compute is being built for latency-sensitive commercial services. Low-power
silicon is being built for connected devices. Harvesting circuits are being
refined for industrial sensing and implants. Launch capacity is being built
for satellite constellations. Inference capacity is being built for a market
that has nothing to do with spatial computing.

<Axiom n="5">
  Nobody is funding "ambient-powered computing," and nobody needs to. The
  future described here emerges from the intersection of investments already
  being made, not from a deliberate program to create it. That is the
  strongest reason to believe it happens, and also the reason it will arrive
  without an announcement.
</Axiom>

## 17. What can be built today

A vision that requires everyone to wait is not a vision. It is a forecast,
and forecasts do not get funded. So here is the practical question: what can
be built and sold profitably in the next three years that also moves this
architecture forward? Every item below passes one test:

> It has a paying customer who does not care about this essay.

That constraint is not a compromise. It is the mechanism. Infrastructure
gets built when each layer is independently viable, and the businesses that
end up owning a transition are almost always the ones that were solving a
boring problem for real money while the transition happened around them.

**Shipping now, with customers today.** Ultra-low-power perception silicon —
sensors and front ends that detect, classify, and wake at microwatt averages
— sells into industrial condition monitoring, automotive occupant sensing,
hearables, and security, and every design win drives Curve One. Battery-free
sensing for buildings and logistics is the clearest wedge for ambient power:
commercial buildings, cold chain, warehouses, and retail shelf systems deploy
sensors by the thousand, and the dominant lifetime cost is a technician
walking around replacing batteries — harvested-power sensors sell on
maintenance economics alone. Split-compute wearables for enterprise — glasses
that offload rendering and inference to a nearby node — sell into field
service, warehousing, surgical support, and inspection, where enterprise
tolerates a tether consumers will not. Latency-critical edge inference nodes
— micro data centers placed near population density — sell to cloud gaming,
live production, industrial vision, and autonomous systems.

**The strategic middle: highest leverage, least crowded.** The adaptive
representation layer — the software that decides, continuously, what to
transmit and what to reconstruct — is the most valuable unbuilt thing in the
entire architecture, and it already has customers in remote robot and drone
teleoperation, cloud rendering, and volumetric telepresence, all currently
solving it badly and privately inside their own stacks. Build it well,
publish the benchmarks, and it becomes the standard by default. Persistent
spatial scene services — a maintained, queryable, up-to-date representation
of a real building — sell today to construction, facilities management,
insurance, and robotics integrators. Infrastructure-side robot autonomy sells
warehouse and factory operators a reduction in cost per robot by moving
perception and planning off the robot and into the building. Compute as a
building system — specification, integration, and operation of inference
capacity as part of a building's design — sells the way structured cabling
and building management systems are sold: low-margin, unfashionable, and the
reason it happens on schedule.

**Capital-intensive, fundable now, returns later.** Power beaming for places
wiring cannot reach — remote installations, off-grid sensing, drone
endurance, defense logistics — has budgets today and no good alternative.
Orbital power *components*, not orbital power stations: lightweight
high-efficiency photovoltaics, thermal management, phased-array
transmitters, deployable structures, ground rectennas. Every one has a
satellite, defense, or terrestrial customer today.

**What not to build.** A fully self-contained consumer headset is the
gramophone with a better needle — the constraint is architectural, and no
amount of silicon or industrial design solves it from inside the device. A
battery breakthrough as the load-bearing assumption is a bet against thirty
years of evidence. An ambient power company with no anchor customer dies in
the gap between demonstration and infrastructure — that gap has killed nearly
everyone who has tried.

## 18. Research agenda

**On device power.** How low can the power draw of a fully capable
sensing-and-display wearable realistically fall, given optimal duty cycling
and an offloaded compute path? What is the true floor for always-on
perception?

**On representation.** What representation minimizes total system energy for
spatial communication, and how does the optimum shift with available
bandwidth? How do you build a negotiation layer that decides, per object and
per frame, whether to transmit or reconstruct?

**On placement.** Where should micro inference nodes be positioned to
minimize latency and cost across a real population density? What is the
economic crossover point between local and distributed inference?

**On energy.** What is the minimum viable ambient power density for a useful
class of devices, and what does it cost to deliver at room, building, and
city scale? What regulatory framework governs diffuse wireless energy —
exposure limits, spectrum allocation, interference, and liability?

**On embodied systems.** What is the orientation-averaged effective
collecting area of a conformal rectenna surface across a realistic
locomotion cycle, measured rather than modeled? Where does the energy balance
close first — which class of machine, in which environment, becomes
wirelessly sustainable soonest?

**On the outer layer.** How should orbital and terrestrial power
infrastructure coexist technically and economically? What receiver
architecture serves both?

None of these have settled answers. Several are dissertations. A few are
companies.

## 19. Conclusion — computing leaves the device

This essay does not predict a gadget. It describes an architecture, and
architectures outlive the products built on them.

The history of computing has been a history of moving work to wherever it is
cheapest to perform. Mainframe to desk, desk to pocket, pocket to cloud,
cloud to edge. Each move looked like a product decision and was actually a
placement decision, and each one was obvious in retrospect and contested at
the time. The next move is to stop asking *how powerful can the device
become* and start asking a different question:

> Which parts of computing belong on the body, in the room, in the city, and
> eventually in orbit?

That question is the whole argument. Once it replaces the old one, a set of
independently developing trends stops looking coincidental. Falling device
power, improving harvesting, decentralizing compute, and cheapening launch
are not four unrelated stories. They are four sides of one transition, and
they are converging whether or not anyone plans it.

Which returns us to where this essay started. Standing inside a recording
instead of watching it, meeting someone in a place that did not exist an
hour ago, wearing something that never charges — none of that is a hardware
wish. It is what falls out of answering the placement question correctly.
Every element of it is downstream of moving computation off the body and
energy off the device. Get the architecture right and the experience is a
consequence rather than a goal.

The Kardashev Type I threshold sits roughly four orders of magnitude above
our current planetary energy use. We do not close that gap by improving
batteries. We close it by learning to treat energy the way we already treat
bandwidth and clean water: as ambient, distributed, and universally
available rather than as a resource each device hoards for itself.

The device becomes lighter. The environment becomes smarter. Computation
dissolves into the spaces we inhabit, and the battery — the last unsolved
systems problem — stops being a problem by ceasing to be a requirement.

That is not a prediction about hardware. It is a question about placement,
and the answer is already being built by people who think they are building
something else.
