Wireless power transmission delivers usable electrical energy across open
space, with no wire, plug, or contact between source and receiver. The
physics has been settled since 1975, when Brown and JPL beamed 30 kW across
1.54 km at Goldstone and recovered it as DC at over 80 percent conversion
efficiency. Fifty years later that demonstration has never been meaningfully
surpassed. What has changed is not whether it works. It is whether it can
become infrastructure.

## 1. The technology and the vision

The technology splits by range. Near-field inductive coupling — the Qi
charger under every phone — works to a few centimetres. Far-field radiative
transfer, where energy leaves as a propagating wave and is captured by a
rectenna, is unbounded in range and is the only regime that supports a
genuine infrastructure vision. Everything else is a better cable.

**Terrestrial.** Rooms and buildings carry ambient transmitters the way they
carry lighting and Wi-Fi. Power fills the volume rather than targeting a
device. Anything with an antenna and a rectifier harvests continuously on
entry, with no pairing and no negotiation. Adding a receiver costs the
infrastructure nothing — the system scales like broadcast, not like a
distribution grid.

**Orbital.** A collector in geostationary orbit sits in sunlight over 99
percent of the year, receiving 1,361 W/m² undiminished by atmosphere, against
a terrestrial capacity factor of 15–25 percent. The energy converts to
microwave, beams to a ground rectenna, and rectifies back to grid power —
through weather, through night, continuously. Glaser proposed this in 1968;
the NASA/DOE Reference System worked it out in full by 1979. It died on
launch cost, not physics. Caltech's MAPLE demonstrator closed the loop
end-to-end from orbit in 2023.

What this buys, in both cases: power becomes a property of a *space* rather
than a property of a *link*. Storage stops being sized for the interval
between charges and shrinks to the interval between harvests. The connector —
the most common point of mechanical failure in any device — disappears as a
design constraint. Energy reaches sealed, moving, and remote places copper
cannot economically follow.

None of this requires new physics. Every element has been demonstrated at
some scale. The reason it is not yet infrastructure is the subject of the
next section.

## 3. Design philosophy

Sixty years of wireless power research optimized the wrong side of the
equation. The field decided early that the transmitter was the variable and
the device was a fixed demand. Everything that followed is downstream of
that single, catastrophic choice. If the device needs what it needs, the
only remaining lever is to deliver energy more precisely — so the field
built tighter beams, larger apertures, faster tracking loops, and spent
careers shaving beam collection efficiency from 88 percent to 91. Solar power
satellite architectures are retrodirective by default: the ground station
emits a pilot, an orbital array of several billion elements phase-conjugates
it, and the whole structure continuously corrects against its own thermal
deformation to hold lock on a ten-kilometre rectenna. Terrestrial systems
copy the pattern at room scale. The receiver announces its location. The
transmitter aims.

This approach is a failure of imagination as much as a failure of taste.
Commercial systems already ping devices up to a hundred times a second so
power can be beamed back along the exact path the ping arrived on — which
requires the transmitter to maintain a live model of the room, the
reflectivity of every surface, and the position of every person standing in
it, so the beam can route around bodies on its way to your pocket. The
obvious extension has already been patented: derive the user's position from
the trajectory of the power beam more accurately than any conventional
indoor positioning system, then use that position to gate what the device is
allowed to do. That was written up as a feature.

<MarginNote label="the honest description" tone="spec">
  Walk across your living room and a beam walks with you, re-aiming a hundred
  times a second, because the infrastructure cannot give you anything until
  it knows exactly where you are. It takes the one technology that should
  feel like abundance and converts it into continuous location tracking,
  then congratulates itself on the efficiency number on the chart. The
  orbital version is the same idea at planetary scale — a one-kilometre
  transmitting aperture aimed at a ten-by-thirteen-kilometre field of fenced,
  excluded ground hardware, built to force diffraction back into a box after
  physics already said it doesn't fit.
</MarginNote>

Point-to-point delivery is the architecture of scarcity. It is correct only
if the receiving side needs a large, specific quantity of power at a
specific place. Under that condition every joule is worth chasing, tracking
earns its complexity, and the tenth device in a room genuinely costs the
infrastructure something, because the infrastructure has to find it, aim at
it, and hold the lock.

Six of the eight binding constraints from Section 2 — spreading,
diffraction, atmospheric absorption, ionospheric nonlinearity, exposure
limits, multipath — are geometry, atmosphere, and biology. None of them move.
The field committed its entire research budget to pushing against the
immovable half of the problem and treated the movable half as somebody else's
department.

<Axiom n="1">
  The movable half is device demand. It is the only term in the inequality
  that was ever free, and it is collapsing right now by orders of magnitude
  — semiconductor scaling, microwatt-class sensing, and the migration of
  compute off the device into infrastructure that hyperscalers are already
  financing for reasons that have nothing to do with power delivery. Once
  that number falls far enough, tracking has nothing left to justify it.
</Axiom>

## 4. Let it spread

My alternative is to stop fighting diffraction and start using it.

A transmitter radiates. The wave spreads, bends, reflects off walls, and
fills the volume it is in. The field treats this as the enemy — the thing
phased arrays exist to suppress, the reason apertures have to be kilometres
wide. I treat it as the delivery mechanism. Saturate a space with low-intensity
RF at the regulatory exposure limit, and every device inside that space
harvests continuously from the field it is already sitting in. No pilot
signal. No location reporting. No aiming. No handshake. No pairing, docking,
or charging behaviour of any kind. A device inside the coverage volume is
powered, and a device outside it is not, and that is the entire user model.

This is how Wi-Fi works, and the parallel is exact at the architectural
level. An access point does not negotiate a beam to your laptop. It radiates
into a room, most of the energy lands on walls and floor and people, and the
fraction that reaches your antenna is enough. Nobody has ever described
Wi-Fi as wasteful. Coverage is the product; the discarded majority is the
cost of never having to think about it.

### 4.1 What abandoning the beam actually costs

Delivered power density is governed by Friis:

$$
P_r = P_t \, G_t \, G_r \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^{2} \eta
$$

Every term is fixed by the deployment except transmitter gain, $G_t$. Gain
is the whole of what tracking buys, and giving it up is the whole of what
this architecture pays. A ceiling-mounted phased array with a 0.5 m aperture
at 2.45 GHz achieves $G_t = 4\pi A / \lambda^2 \approx 211$, or 23 dBi. A
transmitter shaped to flood a room downward operates around $G_t \approx 3$,
or 5 dBi.

**The cost of coverage is a factor of roughly 70 — about 18 dB of delivered
power density, gone.** There is no engineering around this. It follows from
the definition of gain. Concentration and coverage are the same quantity
read in opposite directions.

The honest complement: indoor beam tracking never delivered the gain its
advocates imply. A 0.5 m aperture at 2.45 GHz focuses, at 3 m range, to a
spot of diameter $2.44\lambda d / D \approx 1.8$ m — the tracked beam
indoors is already a body-sized blob. Sixty years of tracking machinery, a
live 3D model of your home, and a hundred position pings per second buy 18
dB and a focal spot wider than the person standing in it.

### 4.2 The arithmetic of a saturated room

Fix the ceiling first. The FCC caps general-public maximum permissible
exposure at 1 mW/cm² — 10 W/m² — above 1.5 GHz, and ICNIRP and IEEE C95.1
land in the same place across the 2–6 GHz band. That is the number, and this
architecture is designed to sit at it.

Holding 10 W/m² across a room requires $P_t = S \cdot 4\pi d^2 / G_t$. At
$d = 5$ m with $G_t = 3$: **roughly 1.05 kW radiated per room.** That number
deserves to be stated plainly, because it is where the Wi-Fi analogy breaks.
A Wi-Fi access point radiates 0.1 W. An ambient power layer at the regulatory
ceiling radiates ten thousand times more. The analogy holds for topology and
fails completely for energetics.

On the receiving side, effective aperture is $A_e = G_r \lambda^2 / 4\pi$ —
set by wavelength, not by device size:

Lower frequency collects an order of magnitude more per element, which is
why every serious ambient system converges on the sub-gigahertz bands. Three
corrections keep this honest. Rectification improves at this density —
incident power of 10 W/m² is 1 mW/cm², the regime where Schottky rectennas
run 50–70 percent rather than the 30 percent they manage in ambient
scavenging; operating at the regulatory ceiling buys efficiency as well as
raw power, and the gain compounds. At the edge of a coverage volume, where
density falls to 1 W/m², efficiency drops back to 30–50 percent and
harvested power falls by roughly a factor of twelve. The effective aperture
in that table is available only to an antenna of roughly λ/2 physical scale
— 16 cm at 900 MHz; shrink the structure below that and the Chu–Harrington
limit takes the efficiency back. The receiving antenna, not the chip, sets
the harvest. And 10 W/m² is the legal maximum for continuous general-public
exposure, which means a room designed to this figure holds its occupants at
the regulatory ceiling permanently — compliant, but with zero margin; any
deployment that wants headroom pays for it linearly in delivered power.

### 4.3 Where the inequality stands today

The condition for battery-free operation is one line:

$$

At the regulatory ceiling the picture inverts. Every low-power device class
closes outright, and the wearable tier — hearables and watches, which the
field has always treated as years away — closes today on physics that
already exists. What remains open is the top tier: glasses and phones,
missing by roughly one order of magnitude rather than the three the 1 W/m²
case implied. One order of magnitude is a gap that gets closed by the
receiving side within a normal product cycle. Sixty years of RF engineering
read that gap and concluded the field must get stronger. I read it and
conclude the devices must get smaller in their demands — and the reason I am
confident is that the entire semiconductor and data-centre industry is
already closing it, at a scale no wireless power programme could ever fund,
for reasons that have nothing to do with power delivery.

## 5. Two kinds of device

Why does a device need power at all? Not how much, not how efficiently —
why. Strip away the product category, the brand, the form factor, and ask
what the electricity is actually being spent on. There are only two answers,
and everything in this essay depends on them being the only two.

A device spends energy to move information — capture it, process it,
transmit it, or present it to a person. Or it spends energy to move matter —
its own body, a payload, a joint, a wheel, a limb. That is the complete
list, and the two obey completely different physics.

### 5.1 The question experts skip: does this have to happen here?

Before splitting by what a function costs, split by *where it must occur*.
Take a pair of AR glasses apart: cameras, inertial sensors, microphones, a
radio, a processor running perception and rendering, a display. Now ask each
one whether it has to be on the face.

The cameras do — the photons arrive at that position and nowhere else. The
microphones do. The inertial sensors do — they measure the motion of that
specific head. The display does, because the light has to enter that pupil.
The radio does, because something has to talk. **The processor does not.**
Bits have no location; a matrix multiply produces the same result in a
wearable, in a cabinet down the street, or in a data centre in another
state. The only thing binding computation to the place the data was
captured is latency, and latency is a distance problem with a distance
solution. The single most power-hungry function in a modern wearable is also
the only one with no reason to be there.

### 5.2 Class A: devices that move information

Everything that survives the locality test — sensors, radio, display, and
the minimum logic to run them — forms the first class: glasses, hearables,
watches, tags, badges, cameras, and every wearable form factor not yet
invented. The cost structure is bounded from below by Landauer's limit:
erasing one bit at room temperature costs about $3 \times 10^{-21}$ joules.
Real silicon operates three to six orders of magnitude above that floor,
which means the information side of computing has thousands to millions of
times of theoretical headroom remaining, and has been descending it for
fifty years.

Once compute is relocated, something unexpected becomes the dominant load:
the display. Getting usable brightness into a pupil through a waveguide that
throws away most of the light costs hundreds of milliwatts today, and that
cost is set by optical efficiency and human photoreceptors rather than
transistor scaling. Once compute leaves, the display is the floor. Anyone
building toward a battery-free wearable should be spending their attention
on waveguide efficiency and emitter physics. Almost nobody is.

### 5.3 Class B: devices that move matter

The second class does work against a physical field: humanoids, mobile
robots, manipulators, drones, actuated anything. Lifting one kilogram by one
metre costs 9.81 joules — the same number in 1600 and in 2600. There is no
Landauer headroom here, no scaling curve, no process node. Motors already
convert electricity to torque at better than 90 percent efficiency, which
means the entire remaining optimisation space on the conversion side is a
single-digit percentage.

A second question, and it is the one that matters most: on level ground,
what is the theoretical cost of walking? Zero. A passive dynamic walker
descends a shallow slope using nothing but gravity and its own geometry,
indefinitely, with no motors — locomotion on flat ground is a cycle of
falling and catching, and the energy released in the fall is available to
fund the catch. Nothing in physics requires a walking machine to burn energy
the way current ones do. Real machines burn it anyway, because they
brute-force every joint with an oversized motor and dissipate the
recoverable energy as heat in the brakes. A humanoid today draws several
hundred watts to over a kilowatt while walking. A human of the same mass
spends a fraction of that. The gap between them is engineering waste rather
than physical necessity, and it is the largest unclaimed efficiency in
robotics.

So Class B splits again: an irreducible part — real work done against
gravity, lifting, climbing, accelerating a payload — and a recoverable part
— the cost of locomotion itself, which current designs pay in full and
should not.

Class A has enormous headroom and an industry spending hundreds of billions
of dollars descending into it for unrelated reasons. Class B has essentially
none on the conversion side, and its only real lever is refusing to waste
energy it could have recovered. Measured against the field from Section 4 —
a room at the FCC ceiling delivering roughly 90 to 125 mW to a sub-gigahertz
receiver — **Class A is one order of magnitude away, and the gap is closing
on its own. Class B is three to four orders away, and no amount of
semiconductor progress touches it.**

### 5.5 The recursion nobody talks about

How much of a robot's energy is spent carrying the robot's own power supply?
A humanoid with a 1.5 kWh pack carries roughly 6 kg of cells, 8 to 10 kg with
housing and thermal management. On a 70 kg machine that is over ten percent
of total mass, and the cost of moving a legged system scales with mass.
Ten-plus percent of every joule is spent transporting the thing that stores
the joules — before accounting for the compute stack it also carries and the
cooling that stack demands. The battery is a passenger that charges rent.
Every improvement in energy density is partly consumed by the mass of the
improvement itself, which is why endurance figures move so slowly despite
genuine chemistry progress.

### 5.6 What follows

Class A is solved by relocation. Move compute off the body, leave the
functions physics pins in place, and the demand curve walks down to meet an
ambient field. Nothing more is required. Class B cannot be solved by
relocation, because gravity does not care where the inference happened. It
needs an architecture built specifically around the fact that its load is
enormous, intermittent, and irreducible. Section 6 takes Class A to its
conclusion. Class B gets its own answer in Section 8, and it is the more
interesting of the two.

## 6. The device this is for

Today's wearables and phones are the wrong target. Writing wireless power
around them is the equivalent of designing lithium-ion packs for 1980s
mainframes — the power numbers never collapse far enough, which is exactly
why every company that tried distance charging of smartphones failed and
retreated to microwatt sensors.

So this is not written for phones or for the glasses currently shipping. It
is written for the thing that replaces the screen. I believe 2D displays are
temporary — not improved, abandoned. When you watch something or join a
call, you will not look at pixels arranged on a plane. You will stand inside
a photorealistic environment rendered around you in real time, with
parallax, occlusion, directional sound, and lighting coherent enough that
your brain stops auditing it and simply accepts the room it is in. Nothing
like this exists today. That is the point — this class of device is being
defined right now, before its architecture hardens, which makes it the only
category where the power question is still open.

## 7. The architecture of a rendered world

Before any of the architecture, the objective stated in one line:

<Axiom n="2">
  Everything in this section exists to drive the power draw of the head-worn
  device low enough that a room can supply it. Compute leaving the device,
  nodes in buildings, choosing what to send over the air — each is a method
  for removing work from something that has no battery. Section 4 gave the
  number: a room at the FCC ceiling delivers roughly 100 mW to a sub-gigahertz
  receiver. So the spec for the entire immersive stack is: build a device
  that stands inside a rendered world on 100 milliwatts.
</Axiom>

The first five lines add to under 60 mW. They fit, and have fit for years.
The last three are the entire problem, and they fail in different ways.
Rendering fails by a factor of thirty. Radio fails or passes depending on a
decision nobody has made yet. Display fails by a factor of two or three and
is a physics problem, not an engineering one.

### 7.2 Rendering: the easy one

Rendering is the largest line in the table and the only one with no reason
to be on the head. Section 5 established why — bits have no location. A
frame rendered in a cabinet down the street is identical to one rendered
against someone's forehead, except that the cabinet is plugged into a wall
and can be cooled with a fan. **Removing rendering and perception from the
device cuts its draw by roughly 95 percent.** Nothing else on the list comes
close. There is no silicon breakthrough required and no new physics
involved — it is a decision about where a chip sits, and the wearable
industry made that decision wrong by copying the smartphone without asking
why. That single move takes the device from watts to hundreds of
milliwatts. It is the difference between needing a battery and not.

### 7.3 Radio: the decision that decides everything

Once rendering moves off, something has to cross the air, and the radio
bill is set by one thing: how many bits. A good short-range radio costs
roughly 1 to 10 nanojoules per bit. Multiply that out:

The question of what format the spatial internet uses is a power question
wearing a costume. Send pixels and the radio alone consumes more than the
entire harvest. Send a description of the scene and let the receiver rebuild
it, and the radio nearly disappears — while the rebuilding cost lands right
back on the device you were trying to keep empty. Both extremes fail. The
rule I would build to:

> Send a thing only when sending it costs the device less energy than
> recalling or reconstructing it.

A wall that has not moved in an hour is already in memory — recall it. A
texture the device has seen a thousand times is a pointer, not a payload. A
face mid-expression gets sent at full fidelity, because generating a face is
expensive and untrustworthy. Every object, every frame, gets that decision
made for it against one budget. Nobody has built this layer. It matters more
than any codec, because it is the difference between a radio that fits
inside 100 mW and one that does not.

### 7.4 Where the compute goes, and why distance is a power problem

Spatial presence breaks if motion-to-photon latency exceeds roughly 20
milliseconds. Spend that budget: sensor readout 2–5 ms, uplink 1–2 ms,
inference and render 5–8 ms, downlink 1–2 ms, display scanout 2–5 ms.
Almost nothing is left for travel time. Light in fibre covers about 200,000
km per second, so a 1,000 km round trip costs 10 ms before a single packet
is switched or queued. Hyperscale data centres are built where land and
power are cheap, which is a long way from where people live. The distance
does not negotiate.

A node that is too far away forces the device to compensate locally —
predicting head motion, warping the last frame, holding a local scene cache,
patching what arrived late. Every one of those is compute back on the head,
and compute on the head is the thing we just removed. Latency failure
converts directly to power failure. So the compute goes into cabinets a few
kilometres out, or into the building itself — not for elegance, but because
that is the only placement where the device gets to stay empty. Sharing
makes it affordable: a wearable's GPU sits idle most of the day and is sized
for its worst second; a neighbourhood node runs near full utilisation,
because a hundred people's activity averages out. Same silicon, a fraction
of the cost per person, and cooling handled by a fan rather than by someone's
forehead.

### 7.5 The room becomes the computer

Push it one step further and the building holds the compute directly,
alongside its electrical, network, and HVAC plans. The reason is mundane and
decisive: **the building is already plugged in.** Grid power at a fixed
location is effectively unlimited, thermally unconstrained, and costs
nothing to carry. A room that senses its own geometry and holds its own
persistent model gives every device inside it a second discount — the
device stops re-deriving what the room already knows. Fewer bits over the
radio, less sensing duty cycle, less local logic. The building does the
work once for everyone in it.

### 7.6 What is left

Run the three moves and the budget closes: rendering and perception
offloaded, 2–5 W down to under 50 mW; adaptive representation over the
radio, 500 mW down to 5 mW; sensors, unchanged, ~30 mW. Total: under 90 mW,
inside the field a room can legally supply.

One line refuses to move. The display still costs 50 to 300 mW, and no
amount of architecture touches it, because the cost is set by waveguide
efficiency and human photoreceptors. **Once compute leaves the device, the
display is the entire remaining problem.** Everything else in this essay is
solved by placement. That one is solved by optics, and it is where I would
put the next decade of attention.

Class A closes here. Section 8 takes on Class B, where none of these moves
work, because gravity does not care where the inference happened.

## 8. The Class B problem

Section 5 drew the line. Class A moves information and closes against an
ambient field within one order of magnitude. Class B moves matter, and the
gap is three to four orders of magnitude. This section earns that number.

Take Tesla Optimus, because it is the most publicly documented humanoid on
earth and there is no reason to argue from a hypothetical when a real
machine gives better numbers.

  saturated room delivers to a thousand devices combined.
</Axiom>

### 8.2 Why the Class A playbook does not apply

Relocating compute does nothing here, because the power is not going to
inference. It is going to torque. A humanoid's perception and planning stack
draws perhaps 50 to 150 watts, and every watt of it can leave the robot the
same way it leaves a pair of glasses — that solves maybe 10 percent of the
budget. The other 90 percent is motors converting electricity into force
against a body gravity is actively pulling down, and gravity does not care
where the inference happened.

Efficiency gains do not close a three-order gap either — motors already
convert electricity to mechanical work above 90 percent efficiency, and
there is no headroom left to shave. And a bigger beam cannot legally exist:
Section 2 already fixed the ceiling at 10 W/m². Closing this gap by raising
transmitted power means delivering kilowatts continuously to a moving target
in occupied space — which Section 3 already named for what it is: not
infrastructure, a directed-energy weapon pointed down a hallway. That option
is off the table, not merely disadvantaged. Class B needs its own
architecture. That is Section 9.

### 8.3 The loop nobody escapes

The robotics industry has a circular problem and has been treating it as a
linear one. A humanoid draws power; peaks are brutal, so endurance is short.
The response is a bigger battery. A bigger battery is more mass. More mass
means more torque at every hip and knee, on every step, forever — which
means more power draw, which means the bigger battery buys less runtime than
its capacity suggested, which motivates a bigger battery. Every iteration of
that loop makes the machine heavier and the return smaller.

**The mass audit.** Optimus Gen 2 weighs 57 kg and carries a 2.3 kWh pack.
At realistic pack-level energy density of around 170 Wh/kg — cells plus
housing, BMS, and thermal management — that pack is roughly 13.5 kg. **Twenty-four
percent of the entire robot.** Nearly a quarter of the machine exists to
feed the machine.

**The loop, written down.** Locomotion power for a legged system scales
roughly with mass. Call that constant $k$; for Optimus at ~375 W average
walking draw and 57 kg, $k \approx 6.6$ W/kg. Let $\rho$ be pack energy
density and $t$ the runtime you want. Then:

$$
m = m_{\text{structure}} + \frac{k \cdot m \cdot t}{\rho}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
m = \frac{m_{\text{structure}}}{1 - kt/\rho}
$$

The battery mass depends on total mass, and total mass depends on battery
mass. This is a rocket equation wearing a different costume, and it behaves
like one:

**And there is a wall.** The denominator hits zero at $t = \rho/k$. For
these numbers that is **25.8 hours.** No battery-powered humanoid at
current energy density and current locomotion efficiency reaches 26 hours
of walking endurance, regardless of how large you build the pack — past
that point the battery is carrying only itself, and adding capacity
subtracts runtime. That wall does not move with better cells the way people
assume: doubling energy density to 340 Wh/kg pushes it to 51 hours — a real
improvement, and still a wall, bought with thirty years of chemistry that
has delivered roughly a tripling in as long.

**Now the dumb question: what is this robot if you take the battery out?**
Delete the pack and Optimus is a 43.5 kg machine. Apply Section 7's argument
and offload the perception and planning stack — the compute board, its
heatsink, its cooling — and remove another 2 to 4 kg. Call it 40 kg.

*The 40 kg figure assumes compute has been offloaded to infrastructure per

Thirty percent off every number in the machine's power budget, from removing
components rather than improving any of them. That is the figure wireless
power should be designed against. Every published analysis of powering a
humanoid wirelessly starts from 57 kg and 375 W and concludes it is
impossible. It starts from the wrong machine. The robot you would actually
build for an ambient field never had the pack in it, and it is 30 percent
cheaper to move as a direct consequence. And the loop reverses direction: in
the battery architecture, every kilogram of storage adds power draw which
demands more storage — the recursion diverges. Take the storage out and the
recursion runs backwards: less mass means less power, which means less
energy to supply, which means less onboard hardware, which means less mass.
The same feedback that made batteries diverge makes a battery-free machine
converge.

### 8.4 What a human costs

Put Optimus next to the machine it was copied from. A human basal metabolic
rate is roughly 100 watts — about 2,000 kcal a day, spread across 1.7 m² of
skin at around 60 W/m². That number is not spent on standing — standing is
free. It buys breathing, blood circulation to every cell, immune
surveillance, continuous tissue repair, digestion, and holding a 37 °C core
temperature against whatever the room is doing. Roughly 70 percent goes to
organ and cellular function, about 20 percent to physical activity, and
around 10 percent to thermoregulation specifically. Inside that same 100
watts, **the brain runs on about 20** — general intelligence, vision,
language, planning, motor control, memory, and abstraction, on less power
than a household light bulb dims to.

The two machines draw the same power at rest. One of them is running an
entire biology on it. The other is standing still. This is the comparison
that should embarrass the field, and constructively: a robot pays none of
biology's overhead — no digestion, no immune system, no cellular repair, no
blood to move, no core temperature to defend — and still burns as much as a
living body doing all of it simultaneously. Its cognition alone costs three
to seven times what a human brain costs while doing far less. So when
someone tells me a humanoid's power draw is near some physical floor, I do
not believe them. There is a 70 kg proof walking around that the floor is
much lower. The gap between Optimus and a human is engineering debt, and
debt gets paid down.

### 8.5 Three variables, one of them locked

Back to the equation everything in this essay runs on:

$$
P_{\text{harvested}} = S \times A \times \eta
$$

Incident power density, effective collecting area, conversion efficiency.
Three terms — take them one at a time and see which ones a designer
actually controls. $S$ is locked: the FCC caps it at 10 W/m², settled by
Sections 2 and 3, biological, internationally harmonised, and not a
lobbying target. Treat it as a constant and design underneath it.

$A$ is where robots have an advantage nobody uses. Section 8.1 quoted a
three-order-of-magnitude gap that came from a wearable's antenna — 177 cm²
of effective aperture. A humanoid is not a pair of glasses. It has roughly
**2 m² of surface**, the same order as human skin, and every square
centimetre of it is a candidate receiving surface if the skin itself is the
rectenna. Two square metres against 177 square centimetres is a factor of
**113.** The robot's own size, a liability in every other part of this
analysis, is an asset here.

  title="Harvest with the robot's own body as the aperture, 10 W/m², 60% rectification"

The honest number is the second one. Effective area is the *projected* area
facing the field, and it changes continuously as the machine turns, bends,
reaches, and walks. Anyone quoting 2 m² for a moving robot is quoting a best
case; real deployment needs multi-directional transmitters and receiving
surfaces distributed so some usable fraction stays illuminated in any pose.
Conformal metamaterial rectennas are what make this term real rather than
theoretical — curved, structural, integrated into the shell instead of
bolted on as a panel. This is an open research problem, and it is the
single highest-leverage unsolved thing in Class B, because it is the only
term with two orders of magnitude sitting in it.

$\eta$ is the other live variable. Rectification runs 50–70 percent at the
densities this architecture operates in, and it improves for reasons
unrelated to robotics. So: one term fixed by law, one term with enormous
unclaimed headroom, one term improving on its own. Which leaves the fourth
quantity, $P_{\text{required}}$. Section 8.3 already took 30 percent off it
by deleting the battery and the compute stack. Section 8.4 established there
is far more to take, because biology does the same job on a fraction of the
budget. Against a 40 kg battery-free machine drawing ~70 W idle and ~264 W
walking, harvesting ~4 W orientation-averaged, the remaining gap is
**roughly one order of magnitude.** That is the real number, and it is a
different problem entirely from the three orders I opened this section
with.

### 8.6 Trickle-spike

Here is the observation the whole architecture turns on: a robot's load is
not flat, and the shape of it is generous. A humanoid spends the
overwhelming majority of its duty cycle near baseline — standing, sensing,
balancing, waiting, moving slowly. Call it 90 percent of the time. The
remaining 10 percent is where the kilowatts live: lifting, catching,
accelerating a joint, arresting a fall. Those events last fractions of a
second. Sizing a power system for those spikes is exactly what produces the
battery brick. And the spikes do not need to be *supplied* by the field.
They need to be *buffered*.

That is the trickle-spike architecture:

> Harvest the baseline continuously from the ambient field. Buffer the
> spikes locally in onboard storage.

Supercapacitors are built for this job — high power density, fast
discharge, hundreds of thousands of cycles, and complete indifference to the
deep-discharge behaviour that destroys chemical cells. Paired with a small
solid-state cell for reserve, they absorb the peak and refill from the
trickle during the far longer intervals between peaks. The energy balance is
one line: over any duty cycle, harvested energy must meet or exceed
consumed energy. Storage decouples instantaneous supply from instantaneous
demand, which means the machine can momentarily draw far more than the
field delivers, provided the average closes.

**This is the reframing that matters. Onboard storage stops being sized for
endurance and becomes sized for the largest single burst.** Endurance comes
from the field. Storage handles transients. Those are different components,
orders of magnitude apart in capacity, and conflating them is what put a
13.5 kg pack on every humanoid in development. This way we can achieve the
best of both worlds — the wireless power dream without the danger of a death
ray, and handling high power requirements without carrying bricks.

### 8.7 Why the gap is worth closing

I should be direct about what this essay is and is not. **This essay does
not solve Class B.** The gap is around one order of magnitude, and closing
it needs conformal rectenna surfaces that do not exist yet, actuation that
wastes far less than current designs do, and orientation-averaged collection
measured in real environments rather than modelled on paper. None of those
are things I can hand anyone — the first-principles questions each one
raises are collected below.

What this essay asserts is that the gap is one order of magnitude rather
than three, that every term in it except the safety ceiling is moving in a
favourable direction, and that ambient-powered robotics is a systems design
problem rather than a physics problem. That distinction is the entire
argument, and it decides whether serious people spend their careers on it.

Here is why they should. A human works roughly 2,080 hours a year. A robot
that never stops to charge, at 95 percent uptime, works 8,322 — **a 4×
multiplier on labour hours**, and it comes from removing the charging cycle
alone: 3× from the shift arithmetic, the rest from holidays, breaks, and
shift changeover that a machine tied to no cell simply does not have.
Today's humanoids run four to eight hours and then go to a dock. Every one
of them is a machine that works one shift and rests two, and the resting is
imposed by a component the machine did not need to carry in the first
place. Remove it, and physical labour stops being metered in shifts. That is
what abundance actually looks like when you trace it back to a mechanism:
continuous, uninterrupted physical work at a marginal cost that approaches
the cost of the electricity, available to anyone, everywhere the field
reaches. That is worth an order of magnitude of somebody's attention.

## 9. Research agenda

**On the transmitter side, where I made you accept a ceiling.** If 10 W/m²
is fixed by biology and not by engineering, what does a room actually look
like once every surface — ceiling, wall, fixture — participates in holding
that density instead of relying on one point source? What does occupied,
furnished, human-filled multipath do to delivered density in practice,
versus the free-space numbers used throughout Section 4? Nobody has
published what a living room with three people and a couch actually
measures.

**On the receiving side, where the whole architecture lives or dies.**
Section 4 fixed effective aperture at λ/2 physical scale — is that a hard
floor, or a rule of thumb inherited from antennas designed for something
other than harvesting? What happens at the Chu–Harrington limit specifically
for a rectifying receiver rather than a transmitting one? Section 8 leaned an
entire order-of-magnitude improvement on conformal metamaterial rectennas
covering a robot's own skin — nobody has published an orientation-averaged
efficiency number for a curved, multi-surface receiver moving through a
realistic gait cycle. Until that number exists, the 4-watt figure is a
calculation, not a measurement. Is 70 percent rectification close to the
physical limit for a diode-based rectifier at 10 W/m², or is that number an
artifact of circuits nobody has optimised for this specific operating point
because nobody needed to before now?

**On the question I keep returning to and never fully answer.** Section 8.4
put a human next to a robot and found the robot losing on baseline draw
despite carrying none of biology's overhead. Is it the actuator topology —
direct-drive motors fighting their own inertia where tendons and elastic
elements would store and return energy the way muscle does? Is it the
control architecture — continuous high-frequency correction where a nervous
system uses sparse, event-driven signalling? Or is it simply that nobody has
had to care yet, because grid power made the question uninteresting? I
suspect it is mostly the third one, and if I am right, the fix is cultural
before it is technical.

**On the load itself.** What is the true baseline draw of a humanoid with
the battery and the compute stack both removed, measured rather than
derived the way I derived it in Section 8.3? My 40 kg, 70-watt figure is an
extrapolation from published Optimus data — someone needs to build the
machine and put a meter on it. What fraction of a robot's peak-to-baseline
ratio is genuinely irreducible — real work against gravity — versus
recoverable, the way a passive dynamic walker suggests most locomotion cost
is? Nobody has published that split for a legged humanoid doing real tasks
rather than walking on a flat lab floor.

**On the floor itself, for both classes at once.** Every number used for
device demand in this essay is a current-generation number — today's
silicon, today's actuators, today's control loops. Given the best actuation
and the most power-efficient computing physics actually permits, not what
ships today, what is the true minimum average draw for a Class A device
doing full spatial sensing and display, and for a Class B device doing
useful physical work? Section 5 gave the governing limits — Landauer for
one, Newtonian work for the other — but a governing limit is not a floor.

**On storage, since trickle-spike only works if the spike side holds up.**
What supercapacitor energy density, at what cycle life, actually closes the
gap for a real duty cycle of lifts, catches, and recoveries rather than the
clean 90/10 split used for illustration here? I named the mechanism in
Section 8.6. I did not size it against a real task distribution, because
that distribution does not exist in the literature yet.

**On the outer layer.** Terrestrial and orbital ambient power are the same
architecture at different altitude, as argued in the earlier essay this one
grew out of. What receiver architecture actually serves both without being
over-built for one and under-built for the other? I have not touched this
seriously here, and I do not think it should be touched seriously until the
terrestrial case closes first.

**The one I think matters most.** If Class A closes this decade — and
Section 7's arithmetic says it does — and Class B closes within the
following one, what changes about how a city is built? Not the power layer.
The rest of it — streets, buildings, labour, ownership, what a factory is,
what a home is. I do not know the answer. I am not sure the question has
been asked seriously by anyone who also understands the physics in Sections
2 through 8, and I think it should be.

## Conclusion — why I wrote this

I have spent ten sections on glasses, rooms, and robots. None of them are the
reason I wrote this essay.

Intelligence scales with energy. Not with data — there is enough, and more
can be generated. Not with clever training tricks — those buy percentage
points, not orders of magnitude. By 2026 the industry arrived at this
without my help: the binding constraint on frontier AI stopped being chip
supply and became electricity. A single training campus now needs 100
megawatts to a gigawatt of dedicated power — a nuclear plant's output, aimed
at one building. Hyperscalers are building their own plants on-site because
grid queues are now the ceiling on how smart a model can get. That is the
tracked-beam mistake from Section 3, replayed at civilizational scale —
point-to-point, exclusive, one dedicated plant per campus.

My claim: the ceiling on energy capture is architectural, not physical.
Physicists put a Kardashev Type II civilization a thousand to two thousand
years out. That number is right about today's tools — human-paced
construction, grid-bound, point-to-point. It assumes the next thousand years
get built like the last thousand. Here is the bridge. $P_{\text{harvested}} =
S \times A \times \eta$ does not know whether it is powering glasses, a
robot, or a data centre. A room at the FCC ceiling and an orbital farm
beaming gigawatts to a rectenna field are the same architecture at different
apertures. Solve ambient and orbital delivery once, and you have not built a
wearable feature — you have built the delivery mechanism for the one
resource gating every further gain in machine intelligence.

Now the loop that collapses the timeline. Section 8.7 gave robots roughly
four times human working hours once they stop needing a dock. Free of
batteries, they do not just do warehouse work — they build rectenna farms
and launch collectors twenty-four hours a day, bounded only by how much of
the field they stand in. More energy trains more capable models. Those
models get pointed at exactly the open problems in Section 9 — rectenna
efficiency, actuation, launch cost — and close them faster than any human
research community would. More energy makes more intelligence; more
intelligence closes the energy problems faster. That loop does not run on
institutional timescales.

I do not think we reach Type II in fifty to sixty years by getting
marginally better at solar panels. I think we reach it by removing the
assumption that energy must be delivered point-to-point and stored locally —
the same assumption I spent this essay arguing against for a pair of
glasses. This essay is the first piece of that argument.

The argument I keep coming back to, underneath the equations, is a smaller
and more uncomfortable one: the field spent sixty years optimizing for
certainty over reach, because certainty was the defensible choice in every
room a proposal had to survive. I think that instinct — reward the failure
that looks like everyone else's failure, avoid the one that is only yours —
explains more of the last sixty years of this field than any physical
constant does.
